<THEOREM_STATEMENT id="1">
For \(p\in[0,1]\), a Bernoulli\((p)\) random variable is a random variable taking value \(1\) with probability \(p\) and value \(0\) with probability \(1-p\). Let \(\mathcal P(p)\) denote the following assertion: for every integer \(m\ge1\), every choice of nonnegative real numbers
\[
w_1,\ldots,w_m\ge0,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{m}w_i=1,
\]
and every family \(v_1,\ldots,v_m\) of i.i.d. Bernoulli\((p)\) random variables, one has
\[
\Pr\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_i v_i\ge p\right]\ge p.
\]

Statement:
The assertion \(\mathcal P(p)\) holds exactly for
\[
p\in[0,\tfrac13]\cup\{\tfrac12,1\}.
\]
Equivalently, the complete set of probabilities \(p\) for which
\[
\Pr\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m} w_i v_i\ge p\right]\ge p
\]
holds for every finite convex weight vector \((w_1,\ldots,w_m)\) and every i.i.d. Bernoulli\((p)\) family \(v_1,\ldots,v_m\) is
\[
\boxed{[0,\tfrac13]\cup\{\tfrac12,1\}}.
\]
</THEOREM_STATEMENT>

<PROPOSITION_STATEMENT id="1">
Let \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\) be i.i.d. real-valued random variables. For \(t\in\mathbb R\), define
\[
L(t):=\inf_{N\ge1}
\Pr\!\left[\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_N}{N}\ge t\right].
\]

Statement:
For every integer \(m\ge1\) and every choice of weights
\[
w_1,\ldots,w_m\ge0,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{m}w_i=1,
\]
one has
\[
\Pr\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m} w_iX_i\ge t\right]\ge L(t).
\]
</PROPOSITION_STATEMENT>

<LEMMA_STATEMENT id="1.1">
Let \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\) be i.i.d. real-valued random variables. Let \(m\ge1\), let
\[
w_1,\ldots,w_m\ge0,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{m}w_i=1,
\]
and let \(u\in\mathbb R\).

Statement:
\[
\Pr\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_iX_i>u\right]
\ge
\inf_{N\ge1}
\Pr\!\left[\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_N}{N}>u\right].
\]
</LEMMA_STATEMENT>

<LEMMA_PROOF id="1.1">
This is the one-dimensional half-line form of Hoeffding's extremal theorem for convex combinations of i.i.d. random variables; see Hoeffding \cite{Hoeffding1955}.

External citation used: W. Hoeffding, \emph{The extrema of the expected value of a function of independent random variables}, Annals of Mathematical Statistics \(\mathbf{26}\) (1955), 268--275.
</LEMMA_PROOF>

<LEMMA_STATEMENT id="2.2">
Let \(0<p\le1/3\), let \(N\ge1\), and let \(X_{N,p}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(N,p)\).

Statement:
\[
\Pr[X_{N,p}\ge Np]\ge p.
\]

\[
\Pr[X_{N,p}\ge \lceil Np\rceil]\ge p.
\]
</LEMMA_STATEMENT>

<LEMMA_PROOF id="2.2">
For \(0<p\le1/3\), the first assertion is the classical binomial mean-tail estimate of Samuels and Jogdeo--Samuels; see \cite{Samuels1965,JogdeoSamuels1968}. In the form needed here, that estimate states exactly that for every \(N\ge1\),
\[
\Pr[X_{N,p}\ge \lceil Np\rceil]\ge p.
\]
Because \(X_{N,p}\) is integer-valued, the events
\[
\{X_{N,p}\ge Np\}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\{X_{N,p}\ge \lceil Np\rceil\}
\]
are identical. This proves the first assertion.

External citations used: S. M. Samuels, \emph{On the number of successes in independent trials}, Annals of Mathematical Statistics \(\mathbf{36}\) (1965), 1272--1278; K. Jogdeo and S. M. Samuels, \emph{Monotone convergence of binomial probabilities and a generalization of Ramanujan's equation}, Annals of Mathematical Statistics \(\mathbf{39}\) (1968), 1191--1195.
</LEMMA_PROOF>

<LEMMA_STATEMENT id="5.2">

\[
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_i v_i=1
\]

<LEMMA_PROOF id="5.2">

\[

\[
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_i v_i
=
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_i
=
1
\]
almost surely. Hence
\[
\Pr\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_i v_i\ge1\right]=1\ge1.
\]
</LEMMA_PROOF>

<THEOREM_PROOF id="1">
The problem is to determine for which probabilities \(p\) the displayed inequality holds uniformly over all finite convex weight vectors. The main positive argument has two reductions. First, Hoeffding's extremal theorem for convex combinations of i.i.d. random variables reduces the weighted problem to the special case of equal weights. In the Bernoulli case, equal weights give binomial averages. Second, a classical binomial mean-tail estimate of Jogdeo--Samuels type says that for \(0\le p\le 1/3\), every binomial average is at least its mean with probability at least \(p\). These two facts are stated precisely in Propositions 1 and 2 and used in Proposition 3.

The remaining cases are elementary. At \(p=1/2\), the Bernoulli subset is uniformly distributed over all subsets, and pairing every subset with its complement gives probability at least \(1/2\). At \(p=1\), the assertion is immediate. To show that there are no other probabilities, equal weights on three variables give counterexamples for every \(p\in(1/3,1/2)\), and equal weights on two variables give counterexamples for every \(p\in(1/2,1)\).

The proof uses two standard one-dimensional results. They are quoted in the precise forms needed below. The first is Hoeffding's reduction theorem for convex combinations of i.i.d. random variables; the second is the binomial mean-tail estimate used to evaluate the equal-weight case.

Proposition 3 proves that \(\mathcal P(p)\) holds for every
\[
p\in[0,\tfrac13].
\]
Proposition 4 proves that \(\mathcal P(1/2)\) holds, and Proposition 5 proves that \(\mathcal P(1)\) holds. Hence
\[
\mathcal P(p)\quad\text{holds for every}\quad
p\in[0,\tfrac13]\cup\{\tfrac12,1\}.
\]

It remains to show that no other probability works. Since \(p\) is a probability, \(p\in[0,1]\). The complement of
\[
[0,\tfrac13]\cup\{\tfrac12,1\}
\]
inside \([0,1]\) is
\[
\left(\frac13,\frac12\right)\cup\left(\frac12,1\right).
\]
For every \(p\in(1/3,1/2)\), Proposition 6 gives an explicit choice of weights for which the desired inequality fails. For every \(p\in(1/2,1)\), Proposition 7 gives an explicit choice of weights for which the desired inequality fails.

Therefore \(\mathcal P(p)\) holds exactly for
\[
p\in[0,\tfrac13]\cup\{\tfrac12,1\}.
\]
This proves the theorem.
